Why Industrial Land Deals Collapse After the LOI

Almost every industrial land transaction in Maharashtra begins the same way — a site visit, a favourable impression, a Letter of Intent, and a round of enthusiastic internal approvals. The trouble rarely shows up at this stage. It shows up four to eight weeks later, when a lawyer starts pulling land records and the picture on paper turns out to be very different from the picture on the ground.

A parcel that looked ready for a factory shed may still be classified as agricultural land, with no NA (non-agricultural) permission on record. A seller who presented himself as the sole owner may in fact hold the land jointly with six cousins, only two of whom have signed the sale agreement. A "clear title" search done informally by a broker may have missed a decades-old tenancy claim, an unreleased mortgage, or a pending litigation that attaches to the land regardless of who currently holds the 7/12 extract.

None of this means industrial land in Maharashtra is unusually risky. It means industrial land is unusually document-heavy, and the documents are scattered — revenue records with the Talathi, mutation entries with the tehsildar, zoning classification with the Town Planning department, encumbrance history with the Sub-Registrar, and, if the land sits inside a notified estate, allotment terms with MIDC. A property lawyer in Pune who works on industrial land deals routinely spends more time reconciling these sources against each other than negotiating the commercial terms of the deal itself.

This is also why industrial land acquisition rarely fits the timeline that project teams draw up before the legal work has begun. A manufacturing unit, warehouse, or processing facility is usually planned around a fixed commissioning date — a client commitment, a lease expiry on an existing facility, or a board-approved capex schedule. Land due diligence, NA conversion, and regulatory approvals do not compress to fit that date; they follow their own statutory timelines, and the only real lever a business has is starting the legal process early enough, and thoroughly enough, that surprises surface while there is still time to fix them rather than after capital has already been committed.

The Cost of Skipping Legal Due Diligence

Unlike a residential flat purchase, an industrial land deal usually involves construction, machinery installation, and regulatory sign-off happening within months of possession. A title defect discovered after construction has begun is not merely inconvenient — it can freeze bank finance, block factory licensing, and in the worst cases lead to demolition or reversion of the land. The window to catch these problems is before the agreement to sell is signed, not after.

The Three Routes to Industrial Land in Maharashtra

Before any due diligence begins, it helps to be clear about which route the land is coming through, because each route carries a distinct legal risk profile.

1. Direct Purchase or Lease of Private Land

This is land owned by a private individual, family, or company, acquired through direct negotiation. It offers the most flexibility on price and location but places the entire burden of title verification, land-use conversion, and zoning compliance on the buyer. Most of the risk discussed in this article concerns this route.

2. MIDC or Industrial Estate Allotment

The Maharashtra Industrial Development Corporation allots developed industrial plots on a long-term lease (typically 95 years) within notified industrial areas — including the Pune belt's Chakan, Talegaon, Ranjangaon, and Bhosari estates. Title risk is lower because MIDC holds clean government title, but allotment comes with its own legal framework: strict conditions on land use, construction timelines, transfer restrictions, and lease-deed covenants that a buyer must understand before bidding or applying.

3. Land Pooling, SEZ, or Government-Facilitated Acquisition

Larger projects sometimes proceed through land pooling schemes, Special Economic Zone allotments, or state industrial policy incentive packages. These routes involve additional layers — state industrial policy conditions, SEZ Act compliance, and in some cases the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013, where government acquisition from original landholders is involved.

Within each of these routes, a further decision has to be made — whether to acquire outright or to lease. Outright purchase offers long-term asset control and is generally preferred where the business intends to hold and depreciate the property, or where the project has a long operating horizon. Leasing — whether a private long-term lease or an MIDC lease allotment — reduces upfront capital outlay and can be the more practical choice for a first facility in a new market, a pilot production line, or a business that wants to preserve flexibility to relocate as it scales. Neither structure is inherently superior; the right choice depends on the project's capital position and time horizon, and it materially changes the legal documentation, stamp duty treatment, and exit options available later.

❌ What Goes Wrong — Route Confusion

A manufacturer negotiated directly with a family holding land inside a notified MIDC influence area, believing they were purchasing freely transferable private land. The land was, in fact, subject to a pending MIDC land acquisition notification under the MID Act — meaning the private sale, even if completed, would not have protected the buyer against the state's acquisition process. The deal was salvaged only after the buyer's lawyer identified the notification and redirected the transaction toward a direct MIDC allotment instead.

The Title Due Diligence Checklist

For private industrial land, due diligence is the single most important stage of the transaction. A property lawyer in Pune conducting this exercise typically works through the following record chain, in this order.

📜

7/12 Extract & Mutation Entries

Confirms current recorded ownership, land classification (agricultural vs. non-agricultural), and any encumbrances noted in the "other rights" column — including mortgages, tenancy, and pending litigation.

Foundational
🔗

30-Year Title Chain

A documented chain of ownership going back at least three decades, tracing every sale, inheritance, gift, or partition, to rule out breaks in title or undisclosed co-owners.

Critical
🌾

NA Order / Land Classification

Verification of whether the land has an approved Non-Agricultural (NA) order under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code, or whether NA conversion is still pending — a step that materially affects timelines and cost.

Timeline risk
👥

Tenancy & Bombay Tenancy Act Exposure

Historical agricultural tenancy claims under the Bombay Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act can surface years after a sale and are among the most common reasons industrial land deals unwind after possession.

High risk
🏦

Encumbrance Certificate

A registered search confirming the land is free of existing mortgages, charges, or attachments — obtained directly from the Sub-Registrar's office, not merely relied upon from the seller's representation.

Bank-facing
⚖️

Litigation & Power of Attorney Check

Court record checks for pending civil or revenue litigation involving the land, and careful verification of any Power of Attorney used to execute documents — a common vector for fraud in family-held agricultural land.

Fraud prevention
✓ What a Proper Search Catches

A logistics company's lawyer discovered, while tracing the 30-year title chain on a warehouse site near Talegaon, that one of eleven legal heirs to the land had never signed a release deed after a family partition in 1998. The remaining heirs had sold the land without her consent. The defect was resolved before the sale agreement was signed — by obtaining her formal release — at a fraction of the cost and delay it would have caused if discovered after possession and construction.

Legal Risks Unique to Industrial Land

Beyond the standard title-search exercise, industrial land carries a set of risks that are specific to its scale, its rural origins, and the regulatory overlay that applies once agricultural land is converted for industrial use.

Ceiling Law Legacy Issues

The Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976, was repealed in Maharashtra in 2007, but its legacy still surfaces in title checks — land that was once the subject of a ceiling proceeding, exemption order, or surplus land declaration can carry unresolved conditions that affect free transferability even today.

Benami Transactions

Agricultural land in Maharashtra has historically seen land held in the name of a family member or associate to work around ownership restrictions or tax considerations. Under the Prohibition of Benami Property Transactions Act, such holdings are vulnerable to attachment by the tax authorities — a risk that transfers to the buyer if not identified before purchase.

Restrictions on Agricultural Land Purchase by Companies

Under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code and related state provisions, agricultural land cannot always be purchased directly by a company or a non-agriculturist without prior permission from the Collector. Structuring the acquisition correctly — including, where applicable, obtaining Section 63 or Section 63-1A permission under the Bombay Tenancy Act before purchase — is essential to avoid a transaction being declared void.

Environmental & Water-Body Restrictions

Land adjoining rivers, canals, or forest boundaries may carry buffer-zone or Coastal/River Regulation Zone-style restrictions, and land within certain distances of protected areas may require additional environmental clearance before industrial construction can proceed — a check that is frequently missed in a purely revenue-record-based search.

Boundary & Encroachment Mismatches

Revenue records describe land by survey number and recorded area, not by physical boundary. It is common — particularly on larger rural parcels that have never been formally surveyed and demarcated — for the area on paper to differ from the area on the ground, or for a neighbouring landholder to have encroached on part of the plot over time. A measurement survey and physical demarcation, cross-checked against the revenue map, is a standard part of due diligence for any parcel large enough to support an industrial facility, and disputes over boundary lines are far cheaper to resolve before construction than after a factory shed has been built partly on disputed ground.

A Clean 7/12 Is Not the Same as a Clean Title

One of the most persistent misunderstandings in industrial land deals is treating a clean 7/12 extract as proof of a clean title. The 7/12 reflects current revenue entries — it does not by itself disclose historical tenancy claims, benami risk, ceiling law legacy conditions, or pending litigation that has not yet been noted in the record. A complete legal opinion requires cross-verification across multiple independent sources, not a single document.

Regulatory Approvals Before You Can Build

Acquiring the land is the first stage. Before construction and operations can lawfully begin, an industrial project typically requires clearances across several regulatory layers — the exact combination depends on project size, sector, and location.

  • NA (Non-Agricultural) order from the Collector, where the land was originally agricultural
  • Zoning confirmation and Development Plan (DP) remark from the local Town Planning authority
  • Building plan approval and commencement certificate from the relevant Planning Authority or Gram Panchayat
  • Consent to Establish and Consent to Operate from the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board (MPCB)
  • Environmental Clearance from SEIAA or MoEFCC, where project scale or sector triggers this requirement
  • Fire NOC from the local fire department before occupation
  • Factory registration and licence under the Factories Act, 1948, prior to commencing manufacturing operations
  • Electricity connection and, where applicable, MSEDCL industrial load sanction
  • MIDC-specific NOCs and building plan approval, where the plot is within a notified industrial estate
  • Labour law registrations — Shops and Establishments, Provident Fund, ESI, and applicable state-specific licences before employing staff

Sequencing these approvals correctly matters as much as obtaining them. Several — such as the building plan approval and the Consent to Establish — depend on each other, and starting construction before the underlying land-use and environmental approvals are in place is one of the most common and expensive compliance mistakes on industrial projects.

Stamp Duty, Registration & Mutation — Completing the Transaction

Signing the sale deed is not the end of the legal process — it is the point where the transaction becomes binding on paper but still needs to be perfected in the government's own records. Three steps typically follow, and each carries its own risk if rushed.

Stamp Duty Valuation

Maharashtra levies stamp duty on the higher of the transaction value or the Ready Reckoner (RR) rate applicable to the land, which varies by taluka and, for industrial land, by the notified industrial zone. Undervaluing a transaction to save on stamp duty is a common temptation on large land deals — and a common trigger for later disputes, because an undervalued sale deed is vulnerable to being reopened by the stamp authorities, with penalty and interest applied retrospectively. A property lawyer in Pune handling the transaction will typically obtain the current RR rate for the specific survey number before finalizing the consideration structure in the deed.

Registration at the Sub-Registrar's Office

The sale deed must be registered within the statutory timeline at the Sub-Registrar's office having jurisdiction over the land. For industrial parcels that span more than one survey number, or that straddle two revenue jurisdictions, registration can require careful sequencing to avoid the deed being rejected on technical grounds — a delay that can, in turn, hold up the mutation entry and, with it, bank disbursement on any acquisition loan.

Mutation Entry (7/12 Update)

Registration transfers legal title, but the buyer's name is not reflected in the revenue record until a mutation entry is processed by the Talathi and confirmed by the tehsildar. Until mutation is complete, the 7/12 extract continues to show the seller's name — which can create friction when applying for utility connections, factory licences, or bank finance that rely on current revenue records. Following through on mutation, and confirming it is free of objections during the notice period, is a step that is easy to overlook once possession has been taken, but it closes the loop on the entire acquisition.

❌ What Goes Wrong — Skipping Mutation Follow-Through

A manufacturer completed registration of an industrial plot and proceeded directly to construction, assuming the transaction was complete. Eight months later, an application for a factory electricity connection was rejected because the 7/12 extract still showed the previous owner's name — mutation had never been finalized, and an objection filed by a distant relative of the seller during the mutation notice period had gone unanswered. Resolving the objection and completing mutation delayed the electricity connection, and with it the factory's commissioning date, by several weeks.

Why This Matters More in the Pune Industrial Belt

Pune's industrial corridor — running through Chakan, Talegaon, Ranjangaon, Bhosari, and Hinjawadi's periphery — has attracted sustained manufacturing, auto-component, and logistics investment for over two decades. That maturity is precisely what makes the legal work more layered here, not less.

Land in and around these belts has typically changed hands multiple times, been the subject of family partitions across generations, and, in several pockets, sits close to notified MIDC boundaries where acquisition notifications, buffer restrictions, or land-use overlaps are common. A property lawyer in Pune working these corridors regularly encounters title chains that intersect with old ceiling-law proceedings, partly-converted NA parcels, and PMRDA (Pune Metropolitan Region Development Authority) development-plan reservations that were not visible from a surface-level record check.

For companies entering the Pune market — whether Indian manufacturers scaling up or foreign investors setting up their first Indian production facility — local legal familiarity with these corridor-specific patterns is not a convenience. It is often the difference between a transaction that closes on schedule and one that stalls in due diligence for months.

Ten Questions to Ask Before You Sign

Before signing an agreement to sell or accepting an MIDC allotment offer, run this quick diagnostic. If you cannot confidently answer "yes" or "verified" to most of these, a legal opinion should come before your signature — not after.

  • Has the 7/12 extract and mutation history been independently verified, not just shared by the seller or broker?
  • Does the title chain go back at least 30 years without unexplained gaps or undisclosed co-owners?
  • Is the land already NA (non-agricultural), or is NA conversion still pending — and has that timeline been factored into your project schedule?
  • Has a tenancy and Bombay Tenancy Act check been carried out, particularly if the land was ever recorded as agricultural?
  • Has an encumbrance certificate been obtained directly from the Sub-Registrar, rather than relied upon from the seller?
  • If a Power of Attorney is being used to execute documents, has it been independently verified as valid, current, and properly registered?
  • Does the site fall within or near any MIDC notified area, and has a check been run for pending acquisition notifications?
  • Has zoning and Development Plan compatibility been confirmed with the relevant Town Planning or PMRDA authority?
  • If purchasing agricultural land as a company or non-agriculturist, has the required Collector or tenancy-law permission been obtained?
  • Has a written legal opinion been obtained that your bank or board can rely on before releasing funds or approving the transaction?
