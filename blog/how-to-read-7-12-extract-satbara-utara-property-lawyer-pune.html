Why the 7/12 Extract Decides More Deals Than the Sale Deed

Every land acquisition in rural and peri-urban Maharashtra — whether it is a family buying a weekend farmhouse near Pune, a developer assembling land for a residential layout, or a corporate acquiring a parcel for an industrial project — eventually comes down to one document that most buyers glance at but few actually read: the 7/12 extract(सातबारा उतारा), universally known as the Satbara Utara.

Buyers often treat the 7/12 as a formality — a paper to be collected, photocopied, and filed alongside the sale deed. That is a mistake. The 7/12 extract is where the real story of the land is written: who legally holds it, who cultivates it, whether it is mortgaged, whether a bank has a lien on it, whether a portion of it is legally unusable, and whether a tenant somewhere in its history has a claim that could surface years after your purchase. A clean-looking sale deed cannot fix a problem that was sitting, in plain Marathi, in the columns of the 7/12 all along.

This guide walks through the 7/12 extract exactly the way a property lawyer reads one before advising a client to proceed with a land acquisition — column by column, with the original Marathi terms in देवनागरी script alongside their English meaning, so that you can recognize these terms on your own document even if you do not read Marathi.

The Assumption That Causes the Most Damage

"The seller showed us the 7/12 and the name matched, so we went ahead" is one of the most common explanations we hear after a land dispute has already begun. A matching name in the ownership column is only one of at least four separate checks a 7/12 extract requires. The other three — Itar Hakk(इतर हक्क) encumbrances, Potkharaba(पोटखराबा) unusable area, and pending Ferfar(फेरफार) mutation entries — are exactly where land disputes in Maharashtra most often originate.

What a 7/12 Extract Actually Is

The 7/12 extract gets its name from two separate village-level registers maintained under the Maharashtra Land Revenue Code, 1966 — specifically Sections 148 to 159, which lay down how the Record of Rights is prepared and maintained in every village. Village Form 7(गाव नमुना ७) records ownership and the rights attached to the land. Village Form 12(गाव नमुना १२) records the cultivation and crop history. Together, printed as a single document, they form what everyone calls the 7/12.

The record is maintained at the village level by the Talathi(तलाठी) — the village-level revenue officer — and each page is authenticated with the signature and seal of the Tahsildar(तहसीलदार), the taluka-level revenue officer. A 7/12 extract exists for every survey number or Gat number(गट क्रमांक) — the unique identifier assigned to a specific parcel of agricultural land within a village.

It is important to understand what the 7/12 is not. It is not, by itself, conclusive proof of ownership. Indian land law does not operate on a "title guarantee" system the way some other countries do — the 7/12 is indicative and presumptive evidence of possession and rights, not an absolute government guarantee of clean title. This is precisely why a property lawyer will always read the 7/12 alongside the sale deed chain, the mutation history, and, for non-agricultural land in cities, the Property Card — the urban equivalent maintained for land identified by City Survey (CTS) number rather than Gat number.

Today, most 7/12 extracts are accessed digitally through the Maharashtra government's Mahabhulekh and e-Mahabhumi portals, which allow anyone to search by district, taluka, village, and survey or Gat number. A digitally signed copy carries a QR code and a verification reference that confirms it was generated directly from the state's revenue database, rather than being a scanned or manually edited image. For any transaction of real consequence, always insist on this digitally signed version rather than a plain printout or a copy forwarded by a broker — a manually altered document is far easier to produce convincingly than most buyers assume, and the digital signature is the safeguard against exactly that risk.

Column by Column — Every Entry Explained

A standard 7/12 extract is organized into a set of numbered fields. The exact numbering can vary slightly by printed format, but the substance is consistent across Maharashtra. Below is how a property lawyer reads through the document, field by field, with the Marathi term shown in देवनागरी script alongside the English meaning.

Field (English / Marathi) What It Records Why a Property Lawyer Checks It
Survey / Gat Numberभूमापन क्रमांक / गट क्रमांक The unique identifying number of the land parcel within the village, sometimes with a sub-division suffix — Bhumapan Kramankacha Upvibhag(भूमापन क्रमांकाचा उपविभाग) — when a larger survey number has been split. Confirms you are looking at the extract for the exact parcel being sold, not an adjoining or similarly numbered one — a surprisingly common source of confusion in rural transactions.
Khate Kramankखाते क्रमांक The landholder's account number, issued under the Khate Pustika (account booklet) rules, used to track all holdings of that person within the village. Links this parcel to the seller's broader landholding record and helps cross-verify identity against the 8-A extract.
Bhogwatdar / Kabjedarभोगवटादार / कब्जेदार The name(s) of the occupant or holder of the land, and the class of occupancy — Class I occupants can transfer land freely, while Class II occupants (for example, land granted under tenancy law) require Collector's permission before transfer. This is the single most important name check on the document — but the occupancy class matters just as much as the name, since Class II land cannot simply be sold like freehold land.
Kul (Tenant)कुळाचे नाव The name of any person recognized as a tenant under the applicable tenancy law — either a contractual tenant or a "deemed tenant" under statutes such as the Bombay Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1948. A tenant entry here can mean a third party has statutory cultivation and, in some cases, purchase rights over the land — even if that person is not the one negotiating the sale with you.
Shetache Sthanik Naavशेताचे स्थानिक नाव The local name traditionally given to the field, often based on its shape, location, or a distinguishing feature — for instance, a field named for a spring or a particular tree. Useful for physically locating and cross-verifying the parcel on the ground and against older revenue records, especially in villages where boundaries have shifted informally over generations.
Lagvadi Yogya Kshetraलागवडी योग्य क्षेत्र The area of the land that is actually fit for cultivation, expressed in Hectares-Ares or Acres-Gunthas. This is the effective usable area — and it is very often smaller than the total area advertised by a seller, once Potkharaba is excluded.
Potkharabaपोटखराबा The portion of the survey number that is unfit for cultivation, split into Category A (वर्ग अ) — rocky terrain, land under a nullah or drain, or land under farm structures — and Category B (वर्ग ब) — land reserved for public purposes such as roads, footpaths, or drinking-water points. Directly affects the usable, and therefore the true saleable, area of the parcel. Covered in detail below.
Aakarniआकारणी The land revenue assessment tax payable on the parcel. A useful cross-check — assessment records are old and stable, and a mismatch here can flag boundary or area discrepancies.
Judi Kinva Vishesh Aakarniजुडी किंवा विशेष आकारणी Judi is revenue historically payable by a holder to whom land was granted by an Inamdar or by the government, under a special assessment arrangement. Where present, this signals the land has an Inam or grant history, which can carry its own restrictions on transfer — worth investigating before proceeding.
Itar Hakk (Other Rights)इतर हक्क Every third-party claim, statutory obligation, mortgage, lease, or liability attached to the land that is not captured elsewhere on the document. The single most consequential column on the entire extract. Covered in detail below.
Hangaam (Season)हंगाम Whether the recorded crop was grown in the Kharif (खरीप) (monsoon) or Rabi (रब्बी) (winter) season. Establishes a recent cultivation pattern, relevant to valuation and to confirming the land is genuinely under active agricultural use.
Pikache Naavपिकाचे नाव The specific crop grown in the recorded season. Relevant for agricultural income assessment and for understanding the land's productive history.
Jamin Kasnyarache Naavजमीन कसणाऱ्याचे नाव The name of the person actually cultivating the land in practice, which may differ from the registered owner or the recorded tenant. A mismatch between who cultivates and who holds title on paper is worth investigating — it can indicate an unrecorded lease, an informal family arrangement, or an unresolved tenancy claim.
A Note on the Devnagari Script

The Marathi terms above are shown in देवनागरी script deliberately, not just transliterated into English letters. Revenue records, court filings, and the original 7/12 document itself are in Marathi — recognizing the actual script helps you locate the corresponding entry on your own physical or digital extract, rather than relying entirely on an English-language summary that an agent or broker hands you.

Itar Hakk — the Column That Hides the Risk

If a property lawyer could direct a client's attention to only one field on the entire 7/12 extract, it would be Itar Hakk(इतर हक्क) — Other Rights. This column is a running log of everything attached to the land that does not fit neatly into ownership, tenancy, or cultivation. In practice, it is where the following commonly appear:

  • A bank's charge or mortgage (गहाण) registered against the land as security for a loan
  • A lease (भाडेपट्टा) granted to a third party for a fixed term
  • An easement or right of way benefiting a neighbouring parcel
  • A pending court case or injunction affecting the land
  • Restrictions arising from the land's history — for example, land that was originally granted under a tenancy or resettlement scheme and still carries transfer restrictions
  • An unresolved inheritance claim, where legal heirs have not yet been formally added or removed from the record

Because this column is a running log rather than a single fixed entry, it can accumulate history over decades — entries are sometimes added and never properly cleared even after the underlying obligation, such as a loan, has been repaid. A property lawyer's job here is not just to read what is currently entered, but to verify, through bank no-objection certificates, court records, or a fresh mutation entry, that anything no longer applicable has been formally removed — not merely ignored.

❌ Reading Itar Hakk Casually — What Goes Wrong

A buyer purchasing land near Pune for a farmhouse project noticed an old bank mortgage entry in the Itar Hakk column, was told by the seller that "the loan was closed years ago," and proceeded on that verbal assurance. Eighteen months later, the bank initiated recovery proceedings against the property — the mortgage entry had never been formally cancelled in the 7/12 record, and repayment alone, without a corresponding mutation entry removing the charge, did not extinguish the bank's recorded interest.

✓ Reading Itar Hakk Properly — What Changes

The same situation, handled correctly, involves obtaining a written no-objection certificate directly from the bank confirming the loan is closed, verifying that a mutation entry removing the charge has actually been passed and reflected in a fresh 7/12 extract, and only then proceeding to registration — with the updated, charge-free extract retained as part of the transaction file.

Potkharaba — the Area You Are Not Actually Buying

Potkharaba(पोटखराबा), sometimes written in full as लागवडीयोग्य नसलेले (land not fit for cultivation), is the portion of a survey number that is legally excluded from the cultivable — and effectively the usable — area of the parcel. It is recorded in two categories:

⛰️

Category A (वर्ग अ)

Land that is physically uncultivable — rocky or hilly terrain, land under a nullah or drainage channel, and land occupied by farm buildings or structures.

🛣️

Category B (वर्ग ब)

Land reserved for public purposes — roads, recognized footpaths, and public drinking-water points — which the landholder cannot use exclusively even though it falls within the survey number.

The practical consequence for a buyer is straightforward but frequently overlooked: the total area of a survey number, as advertised in a sale negotiation, is not the same as the area you can actually use or build on. If a five-acre parcel carries a meaningful Potkharaba component, the effective usable land may be materially smaller — and the per-acre price you are negotiating should reflect that difference. For land being acquired with development, warehousing, or industrial use in mind, Potkharaba also has a bearing on layout planning, since Category B land in particular remains earmarked for its public purpose regardless of who holds the underlying survey number.

Valuation Impact Is Not Automatic — Verify It

A meaningful Potkharaba entry should prompt a specific question, not an automatic price adjustment: does the Potkharaba area actually reduce what you can build or use, given your intended purpose for the land? For a residential farmhouse plot, a rocky Category A strip at one edge may be immaterial. For an industrial layout requiring a regular buildable footprint, the same entry can be a genuine constraint. Read Potkharaba in the context of your actual intended use, not in isolation.

What Happens When a 7/12 Is Read Carelessly

Land disputes involving a 7/12 extract rarely stem from a document that was falsified. Far more often, they stem from a document that was genuinely accurate — and simply not read carefully. The Bhogwatdar(भोगवटादार) name matched, the price seemed fair, and the transaction moved forward without anyone tracing the Itar Hakk(इतर हक्क) history back far enough, or without anyone checking whether a pending Ferfar(फेरफार) mutation entry meant the ownership shown was about to change.

The consequences surface at the worst possible time — typically when the buyer tries to resell, mortgage, or develop the land, and a bank, a subsequent buyer's lawyer, or a revenue authority raises a query that traces back to something that was visible in the original 7/12 all along, but never acted upon.

How the 7/12 Connects to Ferfar, 8-A, and the Property Card

The 7/12 extract does not stand alone. A complete land due diligence exercise in Maharashtra reads it alongside three related records:

  • Ferfar (फेरफार) — the mutation register, which records the history of changes to ownership: sales, inheritance, gift, mortgage, lease, partition, and court decrees. Every entry currently shown on the 7/12 originated as a Ferfar entry at some point, and a pending, unconfirmed Ferfar entry means the 7/12 you are reading may be about to change.
  • 8-A Extract (गाव नमुना आठ-अ) — a consolidated register showing every landholding of a particular Khatedar(खातेदार) across the entire village, useful for understanding a seller's total holdings and cross-verifying the Khate Kramank shown on the 7/12.
  • Property Card — the urban equivalent of the 7/12, used for non-agricultural land identified by City Survey (CTS) number in cities and towns rather than a Gat number in a village.

A property lawyer conducting due diligence for a land acquisition will typically request all of these together, precisely because each one closes a gap the others leave open. A 7/12 read in isolation can look entirely clean — clear ownership, no Itar Hakk entries, cultivable area matching expectations — and still sit on top of a Ferfar entry that has not yet been given legal effect, or an 8-A record showing the same seller has pledged a related parcel as security elsewhere in the village. The discipline is in reading all four together as one connected record, not treating the 7/12 as a substitute for the rest.

How a Property Lawyer Verifies a 7/12 Before Acquisition

Reading a 7/12 extract is a specific, repeatable discipline — not a one-time glance before signing. Here is the sequence a property lawyer in Pune typically follows before advising a client to proceed with a land acquisition.

1

Obtain a Fresh, Digitally Signed Extract

Always work from a current copy — obtained directly through the Mahabhulekh / e-Mahabhumi portal or from the Talathi, digitally signed and dated within the recent past — never a photocopy of unknown age handed over by a broker or seller.

2

Cross-Verify Ownership Against the Sale Deed Chain

Confirm the Bhogwatdar name matches the person executing the sale, and trace the ownership chain backward through prior sale deeds and Ferfar entries to identify how and when the seller acquired title.

3

Scrutinize Itar Hakk Line by Line

Every entry — current or apparently closed — is investigated individually. Closed loans and satisfied claims are verified through no-objection certificates and confirmed mutation entries, not taken on the seller's word.

4

Assess Potkharaba Against Intended Use

The usable area is recalculated against the buyer's actual purpose for the land — residential, agricultural, or development — and the price is evaluated against that effective usable area, not the gross survey area.

5

Check for Pending Mutation and Litigation

The Ferfar register is checked for entries awaiting confirmation, and a search is conducted for any pending civil or revenue litigation affecting the survey number, before the transaction is cleared to proceed.

Who Needs This — and When

Understanding the 7/12 extract is not optional knowledge reserved for large developers. Anyone acquiring an interest in agricultural or peri-urban land in Maharashtra — regardless of scale — is exposed to the same risks a careless reading of this document creates.

7/12 Reading Checklist — 10 Questions Before You Sign

Before finalizing any land acquisition in Maharashtra, run this checklist against the current 7/12 extract for the specific survey or Gat number involved. If you cannot confidently answer more than three of these, the transaction is not yet ready to proceed.

  • Is the 7/12 extract you are reviewing current, digitally signed, and obtained directly from Mahabhulekh or the Talathi — not an undated photocopy?
  • Does the Bhogwatdar (भोगवटादार) name on the extract exactly match the person executing the sale deed, and is the occupancy Class I rather than a restricted Class II?
  • Have you traced every entry in the Itar Hakk (इतर हक्क) column, including apparently closed ones, and obtained written confirmation — not verbal assurance — that they no longer apply?
  • Is there any Kul (कुळाचे नाव) tenant entry, and if so, has that person's rights been independently examined?
  • Have you calculated the effective usable area after deducting Potkharaba (पोटखराबा), and does that area support your intended use of the land?
  • Have you checked the Ferfar (फेरफार) mutation register for any pending entry that could change the ownership shown on the current 7/12?
  • Does the survey number on the 7/12 match the boundaries and area you physically inspected, with no unexplained discrepancy?
  • Have you obtained the 8-A extract to verify the seller's complete landholding in the village and cross-check the Khate Kramank?
  • Has a search been conducted for pending civil, revenue, or tenancy litigation affecting this specific survey number?
  • Has the entire record — 7/12, Ferfar, 8-A, and sale deed chain — been reviewed together by independent legal counsel, rather than relying on the seller's or broker's summary?
